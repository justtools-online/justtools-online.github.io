### Guide

#### How words and characters are counted

A **word** is any run of characters between spaces that contains at least one letter or number. That means “well-known”, “don’t”, “COVID-19” and “2026” each count as one word, while a stray dash or ampersand does not — the same rules Microsoft Word and Google Docs use, so your count matches what an editor or teacher will see.

Languages written without spaces between words — Chinese, Japanese and Thai — are split into words with the Unicode word-segmentation rules built into your browser. Hindi, Marathi, Bengali and other Indic scripts, which do use spaces, are counted like English.

**Characters** are counted the way you see them: an emoji with a skin tone, a flag, or a Devanagari conjunct is one character, even though it is stored as several code points. The Details panel also shows the size in bytes (UTF-8), which matters for databases and some SMS gateways.

**Paragraphs** are counted as non-empty lines, as in Microsoft Word. **Sentences** end at a full stop, question mark, exclamation mark or the Devanagari danda (।).

#### How many pages is 1,000 words?

With a 12-point font such as Times New Roman or Arial and 1-inch margins, a single-spaced page holds about 500 words and a double-spaced page about 250. Headings, short paragraphs and larger fonts reduce that.

| Words | Single-spaced pages | Double-spaced pages | Reading time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 250 | 0.5 | 1 | about 1 minute |
| 500 | 1 | 2 | about 2 minutes |
| 1,000 | 2 | 4 | about 4 minutes |
| 1,500 | 3 | 6 | about 6 minutes |
| 2,000 | 4 | 8 | about 8 minutes |
| 5,000 | 10 | 20 | about 21 minutes |

#### How reading time and speaking time are calculated

Reading time uses **238 words per minute**, the average silent reading speed for adults reading non-fiction English found in a 2019 review of 190 studies (Brysbaert). Speaking time uses **150 words per minute**, a comfortable pace for presentations, podcasts and video voice-overs.

Technical material is read more slowly and fiction faster, so treat both figures as estimates. For a timed speech, rehearse aloud once and adjust.

#### Character limits cheat sheet

| Where | Limit | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X (Twitter) post | 280 characters | Standard accounts; every link counts as 23 |
| SEO title tag | ~60 characters | Google truncates by pixel width, roughly 50–60 characters |
| Meta description | ~160 characters | Longer text is usually cut off in results |
| SMS message | 160 characters | Drops to 70 when the text contains emoji or non-Latin letters |
| YouTube title | 100 characters | About 70 are visible in search results |
| Instagram caption | 2,200 characters | Only the first lines show before “more” |
| LinkedIn post | 3,000 characters | Only the first few lines show before “see more” |

#### Understanding readability scores

The **Flesch Reading Ease** score rates text from about 0 to 100 using average sentence length and average syllables per word: the higher the score, the easier the text. The **Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level** turns the same inputs into a US school grade.

| Score | Difficulty | Typical reader |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 90–100 | Very easy | 5th grade (age 10–11) |
| 70–89 | Easy to fairly easy | 6th–7th grade |
| 60–69 | Plain English | 8th–9th grade (age 13–15) |
| 50–59 | Fairly difficult | 10th–12th grade |
| 30–49 | Difficult | College level |
| 0–29 | Very difficult | College graduate |

#### Keyword density and SEO

Keyword density is how often a word or phrase appears as a share of all words. Search engines do not reward a particular percentage, and repeating a phrase unnaturally can hurt. Use the table to spot accidental repetition and to confirm your main topic is clearly present — then write for readers first.

Some exports could be day/month or month/day. The analyzer works it out from the order of the messages; if it can’t be sure, a switch appears at the top so you can pick the right one.

Yes. Messages in any language — Hindi, Hinglish, Tamil or anything else — are counted and analysed. If WhatsApp itself is set to another language, a few labels like “media omitted” may be counted as ordinary messages.

**How is reply time calculated?**
Each time someone replies to a different person within the same conversation, the gap is recorded. The table shows the median, so one late reply doesn’t skew it.

#### Title Case rules used here

Every word is capitalised except articles (a, an, the), short conjunctions (and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet) and short prepositions (as, at, by, in, of, off, on, per, to, up, via) — unless they are the first or last word, or come right after a colon. This matches the common ground of the Chicago and AP style guides for most headlines.

Hyphenated words get a capital on each part (Well-Known), words after apostrophes stay lowercase (Don’t, India’s), and words already written as acronyms or brands (NASA, iPhone) are left alone.

Capitalized Case capitalises every word. Title Case follows style-guide rules and keeps small words like “of”, “the” and “and” lowercase, as in “The Lord of the Rings”.

**Does it work with Hindi?**
Hindi and most Indian scripts don’t have upper and lower case, so they stay unchanged. English words mixed in are converted.

**Can I convert a list of variable names?**
Yes. Put one name per line and choose a programming case. userAccountId, user-account-id and User Account ID all become user_account_id in snake_case.

**Why wasn’t NASA changed to Nasa?**
Words already written in capitals or mixed case are treated as acronyms and brand names and kept as written. Turn off “Keep acronyms and brand names” to change them too. Text written entirely in capitals is always converted.

### How to copy an emoji or kaomoji

1. **Choose a tab** — Pick **Emoji**, **Kaomoji** or **Symbols**.
2. **Search or browse** — Type a word like “heart” or “shrug”, or jump to a category.
3. **Click to copy** — Click any item — it is copied to your clipboard. Paste it anywhere with Ctrl+V (or long-press on a phone).

#### Emoji in HTML and code

Emoji are ordinary Unicode characters, so you can paste them straight into UTF-8 web pages, documents and source code. Where you need plain ASCII, use the **HTML entity** (😀 is `&#x1F600;`), the **CSS escape** (`\1F600`, for `content`), or the **JavaScript escape** (`\u{1F600}`) shown for every emoji in the details panel.

Some emoji are sequences of several code points — skin tones, flags and family emoji join characters with modifiers or a zero-width joiner — which is why their codes list more than one value.

### FAQ

**How do I copy and paste an emoji?**
Click it — it is copied straight away. Then paste with Ctrl+V on a computer, or long-press and choose Paste on a phone.

**What is a kaomoji?**
A kaomoji is a Japanese-style face made from text characters, read the right way up — like (^_^) or ¯\_(ツ)_/¯. They work anywhere text does, without emoji support.

**Why do some emoji look different on another phone?**
Each system — Apple, Google, Samsung, Microsoft — draws emoji in its own style. The character is the same; only the picture differs. Very new emoji may show as a box on older systems.

**Why do flags look like letters on Windows?**
Windows doesn’t include flag emoji, so it shows the two letters that make up the flag (like IN for India). They still copy correctly and appear as flags on phones and Macs.

**Is anything I type sent anywhere?**
No. Search, copying and your recent items all stay in your browser.
