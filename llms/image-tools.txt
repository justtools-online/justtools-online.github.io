### Guide

#### How does image compression work?

Photos from a phone or camera store far more detail than a screen can show. Lossy formats such as JPG, WebP and AVIF discard the detail your eye is least likely to notice — fine texture in busy areas, tiny colour shifts — and describe what remains more efficiently. The quality setting controls how aggressive that trade-off is.

PNG is lossless, so it has no quality setting in the same sense. For PNGs this tool reduces the number of distinct colours (colour quantisation) and then stores the result losslessly. Screenshots, logos and illustrations use relatively few colours, so they shrink dramatically with almost no visible change.

Everything is done with your browser’s built-in image encoders, so the output is a standard file that opens in any app.

#### JPG vs PNG vs WebP vs AVIF: which format should you use?

Rule of thumb: use **WebP** for anything going on a website, **JPG** when a form or older app insists on it, and **PNG** only for graphics with sharp edges, text or transparency.

| Format | Best for | Transparency | Compression |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| JPG / JPEG | Photos that must open everywhere — email, forms, printing | No | Lossy; the universal baseline |
| WebP | Photos and graphics on websites | Yes | Lossy or lossless; typically 25–35% smaller than JPG at similar quality |
| AVIF | Websites that want the smallest possible files | Yes | Often around half the size of JPG, but slower to encode and not supported by every app |
| PNG | Screenshots, logos, text and flat graphics | Yes | Lossless; this tool adds optional colour reduction |

#### How to compress an image to 100 KB, 50 KB or 20 KB

Job portals, exam applications and government forms often cap uploads at a fixed size such as 20 KB, 50 KB or 100 KB. Switch the mode to **To target size**, pick a preset or type your own limit, and every image is compressed to the highest quality that still fits.

Rather than dropping to a blotchy, very low quality, the tool reduces the dimensions in small steps until the image fits, and labels the result so you know. When a form also specifies pixel dimensions, turn on **Resize large images** and enter them first — the tool then lowers quality as far as needed before it touches those dimensions.

Most forms expect a JPG file. If the form says JPG or JPEG, set the output format to **JPG** so the file type matches.

#### Recommended quality settings

- **85–90** — near-lossless. For portfolios and images you may edit again.
- **70–80** — the sweet spot for websites and sharing; most people cannot tell it apart from the original.
- **50–65** — noticeably smaller files for thumbnails, previews and chat apps.
- **Below 50** — only when size matters more than quality; blocky artefacts become visible up close.

#### Why compress images?

- **Faster websites.** Images are usually the heaviest part of a page. Smaller images improve Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), one of Google’s Core Web Vitals.
- **Upload limits.** Forms, marketplaces and content management systems reject files above a size limit.
- **Email and chat.** Gmail allows 25 MB of attachments per message, so smaller photos mean more of them per email.
- **Storage.** Compressing a photo library can free gigabytes on your phone or cloud drive.

#### Private by design

Many online compressors upload your photos to their servers. This one does not: images are decoded, compressed and saved entirely inside your browser tab, and the page never sends them anywhere. You can check it yourself — load the page, switch off Wi-Fi, and compress an image. It still works.

#### What is WebP?

WebP is an image format created by Google that supports both lossy and lossless compression, transparency and animation. The same picture is usually noticeably smaller as WebP than as JPG or PNG, which makes web pages load faster.

#### Lossy or lossless WebP?

- **Animated GIFs** — this tool converts the first frame only, into a still image.

#### Using WebP on your website

Serve WebP to every visitor today — all modern browsers support it. If you must support very old browsers, use a `<picture>` element with a WebP `<source>` and a JPG `<img>` fallback. Converting your largest images to WebP is one of the quickest ways to improve Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), a Core Web Vitals metric.

Windows 10 and 11 can display HEIC only after installing the HEIF Image Extensions and HEVC Video Extensions from the Microsoft Store, and the HEVC extension is a paid download. Converting to JPG avoids installing anything.

- Use the **rule-of-thirds** grid: place eyes, horizons and key details on the lines rather than dead centre.
- Leave a little space in the direction a person is looking or moving.
- Crop before resizing — cropping a large original keeps more detail than cropping a small copy.
- For profile pictures, centre the face in a square crop: most apps show it as a circle.

### FAQ

**How do I crop an image to a specific size?**
Pick a ratio, then type the width and height under **Resize to** — or choose a social media size, which sets both. The crop box keeps the right shape while you move it.

**Does cropping reduce quality?**
No. The crop is cut from the original pixels at full resolution. Quality only changes if you resize the result or save as JPG/WebP — use quality 90 or higher, or PNG, to keep it pristine.

**How do I crop an image into a circle?**
Choose **Circle** under Shape. The square crop becomes a circle with a transparent outside when you save as PNG or WebP.

**Is my photo uploaded?**
No. Cropping happens entirely in your browser, so the photo never leaves your device.

**Can I straighten a crooked photo?**
Yes. Drag the **Straighten** slider until the horizon is level; the crop shrinks automatically so no empty corners appear.

**Which format should I download?**
JPG for photos (smallest files), PNG for screenshots, graphics and transparent circles, and WebP for websites. **Original** keeps JPG, PNG and WebP in their own format; HEIC and other formats are saved as WebP (or JPG).
