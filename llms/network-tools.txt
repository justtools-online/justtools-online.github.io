#### How subnetting works

An IPv4 address has 32 bits. The prefix length — the number after the slash — says how many of them identify the network; the rest number the hosts inside it. A /24 leaves 8 host bits, so 2⁸ = 256 addresses. The first is the **network address** and the last the **broadcast address**, so 254 are usable for devices.

Every extra prefix bit halves the block: a /24 splits into two /25s, four /26s or eight /27s. **VLSM** (variable-length subnet masking) uses different sizes inside one network — a /25 for 100 office PCs, a /30 for a router link — to avoid wasting addresses. IPv6 LANs simply use /64 each; sites usually get a /48 or /56.

### FAQ

**How many usable hosts does a /24 have?**
254. A /24 has 256 addresses, but the first (network) and last (broadcast) can’t be given to devices. In general, usable hosts = 2^(32 − prefix) − 2, except /31 and /32.

**What is a wildcard mask?**
The inverse of the subnet mask: 0 bits must match and 1 bits can be anything. For 255.255.255.0 it is 0.0.0.255. Cisco access lists and OSPF network statements use wildcard masks.

**How do I find the network and broadcast address?**
The network address is the IP with all host bits set to 0 (a bitwise AND with the mask); the broadcast address has all host bits set to 1. For 192.168.1.10/24 they are 192.168.1.0 and 192.168.1.255.

**Can a /31 be used?**
Yes, on point-to-point links between two routers. RFC 3021 lets both addresses be used because a link with two ends needs no broadcast address.

**What subnet size should I use for an IPv6 LAN?**
A /64. Stateless address autoconfiguration (SLAAC) needs it, and a /64 still holds 18 quintillion addresses. Give each site a /48 or /56 to split into /64s.

**What is VLSM?**
Variable-length subnet masking: dividing one network into subnets of different sizes to match each LAN’s needs. Allocate the largest subnet first so every block stays aligned — the VLSM tab does this for you.

#### Why your speed may be lower than your plan

- **Other devices**: TVs, updates and backups on the same connection share the bandwidth during the test.

- **Very fast plans**: above roughly 1 Gbps, the browser and device can become the limit rather than the line.

### FAQ

**How accurate is this speed test?**
It uses the same method as Cloudflare’s own test: precise browser timings, server time removed, and the 90th-percentile speed of many transfers. Results from different test sites differ a little because they use different servers and methods.

**How much data does a speed test use?**
It depends on your speed: a full test uses up to a few hundred MB on a very fast line, less on slower ones. Data saver mode uses at most about 39 MB — the page shows the exact amount after each test.

**What is a good ping for gaming?**
Under 30 ms is excellent and under 60 ms is good for most online games; jitter should stay below about 10–20 ms. Above 100 ms you will notice lag in fast-paced games.

**What is the difference between Mbps and MB/s?**
Speeds are quoted in megabits per second (Mbps); file sizes in megabytes. One byte is 8 bits, so 100 Mbps downloads about 12.5 MB per second.

**Is the speed test private?**
The test connects your browser to Cloudflare’s speed test servers, which see your IP address like any website does. JustTools doesn’t receive or store your results; your history stays in your browser.

**Why is upload slower than download?**
Most home and mobile plans are asymmetric — designed to receive more than they send. Fibre plans are often symmetric, so similar upload and download speeds are normal there.

### Guide

#### Public and private IP addresses

Your **public IP** is the address the internet sees; it belongs to your router or your mobile provider and is shared by every device on the same connection. Inside your home, each device has a **private IP** (such as 192.168.1.5) that only your router knows — find it in your device’s Wi-Fi settings.

Many mobile and some broadband providers in India and elsewhere use **carrier-grade NAT**, so hundreds of customers share one public IPv4 address. IPv6 avoids this: each device can have its own public address.

#### How accurate is IP location?

IP location is a guess from databases of which provider uses which addresses where. The country is almost always right; the city is often your provider’s nearest hub, which can be tens of kilometres away. It never shows your street or house, and a VPN shows the VPN server’s location instead.

### FAQ

**What is my IP address?**
It is shown at the top of this page: your public IP address, which every website you visit sees. Copy it with the button beside it.

**Why do I have two IP addresses?**
One is IPv4 (four numbers) and one is IPv6 (longer, with colons). Many connections now use both; websites that support IPv6 see that one.

**Why does my IP show the wrong city?**
IP location shows where your provider’s equipment is registered, not where you are. Mobile data especially can show a city far away.

**How do I check if my VPN is working?**
Turn the VPN on and reload: the IP, provider and location should change to the VPN’s. Then run the WebRTC leak test — it should not find your real address.

**Does my IP address change?**
Often, yes. Most home and mobile connections get a new public IP from time to time or when the router restarts, unless you pay for a static IP.

**Is it safe to share my IP?**
It reveals your rough location and provider, not your identity. Avoid posting it publicly anyway — use **Hide IP for screenshots**.

#### Why is the owner “redacted for privacy”?

Since the EU’s GDPR came into force in 2018, registrars hide the personal details of most domain owners. You usually see only the owner’s state or country. #### WHOIS and RDAP

WHOIS is the old text-based way of asking who registered a domain; every registry formatted its answers differently. RDAP (Registration Data Access Protocol) replaces it with the same information as structured data, over HTTPS. ICANN made RDAP mandatory for generic top-level domains, and the regional internet registries use it for IP addresses and AS numbers. Some country-code domains don’t offer RDAP yet.

#### Why does my phone show an unknown maker?

Phones, tablets and Windows laptops now use a different random MAC address for each Wi-Fi network, so they can’t be tracked between networks. Such addresses have the “locally administered” bit set — the second digit is 2, 6, A or E — and belong to no maker. That is normal, and the lookup says so.

#### About the IEEE registry

The first half of a MAC address is an Organizationally Unique Identifier (OUI) that the IEEE Registration Authority assigns to manufacturers. ### FAQ

**Why is the vendor unknown?**
Either the address is a random private address (common on phones), or the block was assigned recently. The tool tells you which.

**Can I look up many MAC addresses at once?**
Yes — paste up to 500, one per line.

**Is it safe to generate a random MAC?**
Yes, for virtual machines and testing. Generated addresses are locally administered and unicast, so they won’t clash with real devices’ factory addresses.

**Are the addresses I enter uploaded?**
No. The vendor list is downloaded to your browser and the lookup happens there.

#### Ping, jitter and packet loss

- **Ping (latency)** is the time for a message to reach a server and come back. Distance matters: light in fibre covers roughly 200 km per millisecond, so Mumbai to Frankfurt can’t be much under 100 ms.
- **Jitter** is how much the ping changes from one moment to the next. Below 10 ms is good; high jitter makes calls choppy.
- **Packet loss** means some messages never arrive. Anything above 1–2% causes stutters and dropped audio.

#### How to lower your ping

- Use a **wired (Ethernet) connection** instead of Wi-Fi, or move closer to the router.
- Stop **downloads, uploads and streaming** on other devices while you play.
- Pick **game servers in the nearest region** — the table shows which is fastest for you.
- Restart your router, and ask your provider about congestion if evening pings are much higher.

### FAQ

**What does a ping test measure?**
How long a small message takes to travel from your device to a server and back, in milliseconds — plus how steady that is (jitter) and whether any messages get lost.

**Why is my ping higher here than in the command line?**
Browsers can’t send ICMP ping packets, so this test times a tiny HTTPS request on an open connection. That adds a few milliseconds of processing.

**Which servers are tested?**
Amazon Web Services data centres in 31 cities (the same regions many games and apps run in) and Cloudflare’s server nearest to you.

**Is this the ping to my game?**
It is a good guide: games hosted on AWS in the same region will be close. Game servers elsewhere may differ.

**Does the test use much data?**
No — each ping is a few hundred bytes, under 100 KB for the whole test.

**Is anything stored?**
No. The servers see your IP address like any website does; results stay in your browser.
