### Guide

#### What is a good typing speed?

| Speed | Level |
| --- | --- |
| Under 20 WPM | Beginner — focus on accuracy and finger position |
| 20–35 WPM | Learning — enough for everyday use |
| 35–50 WPM | Average — what many office jobs expect |
| 50–70 WPM | Fast |
| 70 WPM and above | Very fast — professional typists and transcribers |

#### How the scores are worked out

- **WPM** = characters of correctly typed words (with their spaces) ÷ 5 ÷ minutes.
- **Raw speed** counts everything you typed, mistakes included.
- **Accuracy** = keys pressed correctly ÷ all keys pressed.
- **Key depressions per hour** = characters typed per minute × 60 — the measure many Indian recruitment typing tests quote.
- **Exam practice**: full mistakes are left-out, extra or wrong words; half mistakes are wrong capitals, punctuation, or words joined or split. Error rate = (full + half ÷ 2) ÷ words reached × 100.

### FAQ

**How is typing speed calculated?**
Speed is measured in words per minute, where one word is any 5 characters including spaces. Only words typed correctly count towards WPM; raw speed counts all your typing.

**Can I take a Hindi typing test?**
Yes. Choose हिंदी and type with your device’s Hindi keyboard — Inscript, Remington (with a Hindi font input tool) or phonetic. Common words and passages are available, and the exam practice mode works in Hindi too.

**What speed do government exams ask for?**
It depends on the exam and post, so always check the latest notification. For example, SSC CHSL has asked for 35 words per minute in English (about 10,500 key depressions per hour) and 30 words per minute in Hindi (about 9,000 key depressions per hour).

**How can I type faster?**
Keep your fingers on the home row, look at the screen instead of the keyboard and practise for 10–15 minutes every day. Aim for accuracy first — speed follows once you stop correcting mistakes.

**Are my results saved?**
Your recent results and personal bests are saved only in this browser. Nothing you type is sent anywhere.

### How to make an exam seating arrangement

1. **Add your students** — Create one group for each class, section or subject in the session. Type roll-number ranges such as **1-40**, or choose **Paste a list** for roll numbers with names.
2. **Add your rooms** — For each room enter the rows of desks, the desks across and the seats on each desk. The number of seats is shown as you type.

### Guide

#### How the seats are filled

Seats are numbered along a path — down each column of seats, or along each row — and filled in that order. At every seat the planner takes the next student from the group that clashes least with the neighbours already seated, and prefers the group with the most students still waiting, so the groups run out together.

Clashes are ranked: two classmates **on the same desk** are avoided first, then **side by side across an aisle**, then **front and back**, then **diagonally**. Within a group, students keep the order you entered, so roll numbers still run in sequence along the path and are easy to find.

A second pass swaps seats where that removes a clash that was left over. If a clash cannot be avoided — for example when one class is more than half the room — the planner tells you how many pairs remain instead of hiding them.

#### Which rule to choose

| Rule | Who is kept apart | Groups needed | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Side by side only | Desk-mates and side neighbours | 2 | Each column is one class — easy for handing out papers |
| Side by side and front–back | The four nearest seats | 2 | A checkerboard; the usual choice |
| All around, diagonals too | All eight surrounding seats | 4 | The strictest plan, for high-stakes exams |
| Don’t mix | Nobody | 1 | One class after another |

#### How many students fit in a room

Seats = rows × desks across × seats per desk. Take away any seats you mark as not in use.

| Rows | Desks across | Seats per desk | Seats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 4 | 2 | 40 |
| 6 | 5 | 2 | 60 |
| 8 | 5 | 2 | 80 |
| 10 | 6 | 2 | 120 |
| 6 | 5 | 1 | 30 |
| 6 | 4 | 3 | 72 |

- **Attendance sheets** — seat, roll number, name, group and a signature box for each student, room by room.
- **Notice-board list** — for each class, which roll numbers go to which room, so students find their room at a glance.
- **Desk slips** — a label for each seat with the roll number, room and seat number, to cut out and stick on desks.

#### Tips for a fair seating plan

- Seat at least two classes or subjects in every session; a single class can only be spaced out, not mixed.
- For a clean checkerboard no group should be more than half the seats in use. If one is, add a room so that spare seats can act as gaps.
- Keep a few spare seats in every room for late changes, and mark broken desks before printing.
- Use **Shuffled** order for class tests where friends would otherwise sit in roll-number order near each other.
- Print the notice-board list the day before, and the charts and attendance sheets on the day.

**What if a desk is broken or a seat must stay empty?**
Switch on **Mark seats not in use** and tap the seat on the chart. It is taken out of the count and the plan rearranges itself. Tap it again to bring it back.

- **Private and free**: Everything is calculated in your browser. No sign-up, no limits.

### How to balance a chemical equation

1. **Type the equation** — Write the reactants, an arrow and the products, with + between substances: `C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O`. Leave the coefficients out.
2. **Use capitals and digits** — Element symbols need their capital letters (`Co` is cobalt, `CO` is carbon monoxide). Type subscripts as plain digits and charges after `^`: `Fe^3+`.
3. **Read the result** — The balanced equation appears as you type, with the reaction type and a table that counts each element on both sides.
4. **Work out amounts** — In the amounts table, type the grams or moles of one substance to see how much of each other substance reacts or forms.

### Guide

#### How to type formulas

| To write | Type | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A subscript | the digit after the symbol | `H2O`, `C6H12O6` |
| A group in brackets | ( ) or [ ] with a digit after | `Ca(OH)2`, `Al2(SO4)3`, `K4[Fe(CN)6]` |
| Water of crystallisation | a dot or * | `CuSO4.5H2O` |
| A charge | ^ then the charge | `Fe^3+`, `SO4^2-`, `NH4^+` |
| An electron | e- | `Fe^3+ + e- -> Fe^2+` |
| A state | (s), (l), (g) or (aq) after the formula | `NaCl(aq)` |
| The arrow | -> or = (⇌ or <-> for reversible) | `N2 + H2 <-> NH3` |

#### Worked examples

| You type | Balanced |
| --- | --- |
| `H2 + O2 -> H2O` | 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O |
| `Fe + O2 -> Fe2O3` | 4 Fe + 3 O₂ → 2 Fe₂O₃ |
| `C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O` | C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O |
| `Al + H2SO4 -> Al2(SO4)3 + H2` | 2 Al + 3 H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3 H₂ |
| `KMnO4 + HCl -> KCl + MnCl2 + H2O + Cl2` | 2 KMnO₄ + 16 HCl → 2 KCl + 2 MnCl₂ + 8 H₂O + 5 Cl₂ |
| `Cr2O7^2- + H+ + e- -> Cr^3+ + H2O` | Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ → 2 Cr³⁺ + 7 H₂O |

#### How the balancing works

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of every element on both sides, and the same total charge. Each element gives one equation in the unknown coefficients: for hydrogen in H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, twice the coefficient of H₂ must equal twice the coefficient of H₂O.

The tool solves all of those equations together, exactly, and scales the answer to the smallest whole numbers. If there is no solution, the equation cannot be balanced as written — usually a formula is wrong or a substance is missing. If there is more than one independent solution, the equation is really two reactions written as one (for example C + O₂ → CO + CO₂), and the tool tells you so.

#### Digits before a + or − sign

Plain text cannot tell a charge from a subscript: `Fe3+` should be Fe³⁺ but `I3-` is I₃⁻. The tool reads a digit before the sign as the charge on a single atom (`Fe3+`, `Cu2+`, `O2-`) and as a subscript in anything larger (`MnO4-`, `NH4+`); two digits are split (`SO42-` is SO₄²⁻). If that reading cannot be balanced it tries the other, and it always shows how each ion was read. Write `Fe^3+` or `I3^-` to leave no doubt.

### FAQ

**Why does it say an element appears on only one side?**
Atoms cannot appear or vanish in a reaction, so every element must be on both sides. Check for a missing product or reactant, and for typing slips such as `CL` for `Cl` or `NA` for `Na`.

**Can it balance redox equations?**
Yes. Molecular redox equations such as `KMnO4 + HCl -> KCl + MnCl2 + H2O + Cl2` are balanced directly. For ionic ones, include the H⁺ or OH⁻ and H₂O that take part, for example `MnO4- + Fe2+ + H+ -> Mn2+ + Fe3+ + H2O`; charges are balanced along with the atoms. Half-reactions with `e-` work too.

**What does “more than one balanced form” mean?**
Some equations combine two reactions that can happen in any proportion, such as `C + O2 -> CO + CO2`. They have endless valid sets of coefficients. The tool shows one small set and says so; split the equation into its separate reactions for a definite answer.

**How is the limiting reactant found?**
Each amount you enter is divided by that substance’s coefficient. The smallest result belongs to the limiting reactant — the one that runs out first — and it sets how much product forms. The table shows what is left of the other reactants.

**Which atomic weights are used?**
The abridged standard atomic weights from IUPAC’s Commission on Isotopic Abundances and Atomic Weights (H 1.0080, C 12.011, O 15.999, and so on). Elements with no stable isotope use the mass number of their most stable isotope, and the tool notes that the result is approximate.

**Do I need to type the coefficients?**
No — leave them out. If you do type them, the tool tells you whether your version was already balanced, and replaces them if not.

**Does it show the steps?**
It shows the check that matters for marks: the count of each element, the charge and the mass on both sides. The coefficients themselves come from solving all the element equations at once, which is how the algebraic method taught in class works.

### Guide

#### The three rules

- **Aufbau principle** — electrons fill the lowest-energy subshell that has room, in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.

#### Elements that break the filling order

Twenty elements have a ground state the filling order does not predict: Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, La, Ce, Gd, Pt, Au, Ac, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm and Lr. In most of them an electron moves from the s subshell into d, because a half-filled or filled d subshell is slightly more stable — chromium is 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁴ 4s², and copper 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁹ 4s².

These cannot be worked out from the rule; they are measured. The tool shows the measured configuration and, beside it, what the rule alone would give.

#### Why ions lose 4s before 3d

In a neutral atom 4s fills before 3d. Once 3d holds electrons, though, it lies lower in energy than 4s, so when a transition metal becomes a positive ion the 4s electrons leave first. Iron, [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², becomes Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵. Writing Fe²⁺ as [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² — the configuration of a neutral atom with 24 electrons — is the commonest mistake, and Practice mode points it out.

**What is the electron configuration of iron?**

**Should 4s or 3d be written first?**

They describe the final electron added: n is its shell, l its subshell (0 = s, 1 = p, 2 = d, 3 = f), mℓ its orbital and ms its spin. The tool numbers orbitals from −l to +l and puts spin-up electrons in first, the convention most textbooks use; a few courses number them the other way round.

**Why is my answer for Cr or Cu marked wrong in Practice?**

**How reliable are the configurations of the heaviest elements?**

**Which ions can it show?**
Charges from 4− to 8+, which covers the ions met in chemistry. Positive ions follow NIST’s measured ground states; for negative ions the extra electrons are placed by the filling order.

### Guide

#### The half-life formulas

| To find | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Amount left | `N = N₀ × (½)^(t ÷ T½)` |
| Starting amount | `N₀ = N × 2^(t ÷ T½)` |
| Time passed | `t = T½ × log₂(N₀ ÷ N)` |
| Half-life | `T½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N₀ ÷ N)` |
| Decay constant | `λ = ln 2 ÷ T½ ≈ 0.693 ÷ T½` |
| Mean life | `τ = 1 ÷ λ = T½ ÷ ln 2 ≈ 1.443 × T½` |

#### A worked example

Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0249 days. How much of a 400 MBq dose is left after 24 days? The time is 24 ÷ 8.0249 = 2.991 half-lives, so the amount left is 400 × (½)^2.991 = 50.3 MBq — just over an eighth, because three half-lives would take 24.07 days.

And how long until only 1 % is left? t = 8.0249 × log₂(100) = 8.0249 × 6.644 = 53.3 days. A rule of thumb follows from this: after 7 half-lives less than 1 % remains, and after 10 less than 0.1 %.

### FAQ

**What is a half-life?**
The time it takes for half of the atoms in a radioactive sample to decay. It never changes: whether you start with a gram or a tonne, and whatever has already decayed, half of what is left decays in each half-life.

**How do I calculate the half-life from two measurements?**
Use `T½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N₀ ÷ N)`. For example, if a count rate falls from 800 to 100 in 15 hours, N₀ ÷ N = 8 = 2³, so three half-lives passed and the half-life is 5 hours. Choose **Half-life** in the calculator to get the working.

**What is the difference between half-life, decay constant and mean life?**
They describe the same thing. The decay constant λ is the chance per unit time that one atom decays; the half-life is ln 2 ÷ λ; the mean life — the average time an atom survives — is 1 ÷ λ, about 1.443 half-lives. The calculator accepts any of the three.

**Why do radiocarbon labs use 5,568 years instead of 5,700?**
Radiocarbon dating began with Willard Libby’s value of 5,568 years. To keep every published result comparable, laboratories still report conventional radiocarbon ages with it (an international convention since 1977) and then convert them to calendar years with a calibration curve such as IntCal20, which also corrects for the true half-life and the changing amount of carbon-14 in the air.

**Does a sample ever decay completely?**
In theory the amount only halves, so it never reaches zero; in practice a sample is effectively gone after about 50 half-lives, when fewer than one atom in a thousand million million is left. With a small number of atoms, the last ones decay at random times.

**Can I use this for medicines, caffeine or other exponential decay?**
Yes — the formula is the same for anything that halves at a steady rate, such as how a drug is cleared from the body. Leave the isotope empty and type the half-life. Medicine half-lives vary from person to person, so ask a doctor or pharmacist about your own dose.

**Where do the half-lives come from?**
From NUBASE2020 (Kondev and others, Chinese Physics C, 2021), the evaluation of nuclear properties published by the IAEA’s Atomic Mass Data Center. It uses 1 year = 365.2422 days, and so does this calculator.

### Guide

#### Why decay is random but predictable

A radioactive nucleus has a fixed chance of decaying in any second, the decay constant λ, and nothing — not its age, temperature or neighbours — changes it. Which atom goes next is pure chance. But with many atoms the law of large numbers takes over: in each half-life about half of those left decay, and the count follows N = N₀ × (½)^(t ÷ T½).

The smaller the sample, the larger the wobble. The typical difference from the expected number is √(N₀ × p × (1 − p)), where p is the fraction expected to be left — about 16 atoms out of 1,000 after one half-life, but only 1.6 % of the sample instead of 5 % with ten times fewer atoms.

### FAQ

**Can you predict when a single atom will decay?**
No. Only the chance is known: an atom of iodine-131 has a 50 % chance of decaying within the next 8.0249 days, however long it has already existed. That is why the squares change colour in no pattern at all.

**Why does my simulation not match the curve exactly?**
Because decay is random. The count wanders around the expected curve, usually staying within the shaded ±1 standard deviation band. Use more atoms and the wander becomes smaller compared with the sample.

**What is secular equilibrium?**
When a long-lived parent decays into a much shorter-lived daughter, the daughter builds up until it decays as fast as it is made. From then on both have the same activity. Choose the strontium-90 chain and the **Activity** view to see it.

**How does the dice experiment model radioactive decay?**
Each die is an atom and each throw a tick of time. Every die has the same 1-in-6 chance of being removed, whatever happened before, just as every atom has the same chance of decaying. The number left falls exponentially, halving every 3.8 throws.

**Is the simulation accurate for real isotopes?**
Yes. Each atom’s decay time is drawn from the exponential distribution with the isotope’s NUBASE2020 half-life, and the decay chains are checked against the Bateman equations. The only simplification is that branches below 0.03 % in the radon chain are left out.

**Can I use the results for homework?**
Yes. Download the CSV and plot the counts against time, or ln(counts) against time to get a straight line whose slope is −λ. The tool shows its own estimate of the half-life so you can check yours.

| Square planar | XeF₄ | 4 + 2 | 90° |

### FAQ

**Which file formats can I open?**
PDB (.pdb, .ent), mmCIF (.cif), SDF and MOL (.sdf, .mol, V2000 and V3000), MOL2, XYZ, PQR and GROMACS (.gro). You can also paste the text of a file. Files are read in your browser and never uploaded.

**Where do the 3D shapes of small molecules come from?**
From PubChem, which computes a low-energy 3D conformer for most small organic compounds. It is a calculated shape, not a measured one. PubChem has no 3D shape for compounds that are too large, too flexible or contain elements its method does not cover (such as most metals); for those the viewer shows the flat 2D drawing and says so.

**Why are there no hydrogen atoms in my protein?**
X-ray crystallography rarely sees hydrogen atoms, so most PDB entries leave them out. The formula and molar mass then count only the atoms in the file, and the viewer tells you so.

**How do I measure a bond angle?**
Choose **Angle** under Measure and click three atoms, with the middle atom second. For a bond length choose **Distance** and click two atoms; for a torsion choose **Torsion** and click four atoms along the chain.

**What do the purple clouds mean?**
They are lone pairs: pairs of electrons on the central atom that are not in a bond. VSEPR theory says they push the bonds apart, which is why water is bent and ammonia is a pyramid. Switch them off with **Lone pairs**.

**Can I use the pictures in my slides or homework?**
Yes. Use **Picture** to save a PNG. Structures from the Protein Data Bank are free to use (CC0); mention the PDB ID. PubChem asks you to cite it as the source of its data.

**Does it work on a phone?**
Yes. Drag with one finger to turn the molecule and pinch to zoom. Very large proteins can be slow on older phones — the Wire or Cartoon styles are the lightest.

**What is sent over the internet?**
Only what you search for: names, SMILES and CIDs go to PubChem (run by the US National Library of Medicine), PDB IDs to the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The built-in molecules and the files you open do not leave your device.
