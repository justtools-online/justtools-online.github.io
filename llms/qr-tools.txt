### Guide

#### Static vs dynamic QR codes

A **static** QR code — the kind this tool makes — stores your link or text directly in its pattern. It works forever, needs no account and does not depend on any company staying in business. The trade-off: to change the destination you print a new code.

A **dynamic** QR code stores a short redirect link owned by a QR service. You can change where it points and see scan statistics, but it usually needs a paid plan, and the code stops working if the subscription lapses or the service shuts down. For menus, packaging, business cards and payment codes, static is the safer choice.

#### How to make a UPI payment QR code

Choose **UPI payment**, enter the UPI ID (VPA) that should receive the money — for example `yourshop@okhdfcbank` — and the name customers should see. Add an amount for a fixed bill, or leave it empty so the payer types the amount. A note such as an order number appears in the payment description.

Before printing, scan the code with your own UPI app and check that the payee name is correct. Never share a code that pays into someone else’s account.

#### Tips for QR codes that always scan

- **Keep contrast high:** dark dots on a light background. Light-on-dark codes fail in some scanner apps.
- **Keep the quiet zone:** leave the white border around the code; scanners use it to find the edges.
- **Print big enough:** at least 2 × 2 cm (0.8 in). A good rule is one unit of code size for every ten units of scanning distance.
- **Shorter content, simpler code:** long text makes the pattern denser and harder to scan from a distance.
- **Test before you print:** scan with at least two different phones, including an older one.
- **Use SVG for print:** it scales to any size without blurring.

#### Error correction levels explained

Error correction lets a code still scan when part of it is dirty, damaged or covered by a logo. More correction means a denser pattern.

| Level | Can recover | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L | about 7% | Screens and clean indoor use |
| M | about 15% | Most printed codes (default) |
| Q | about 25% | Packaging and outdoor signs |
| H | about 30% | Codes with a logo, or harsh conditions |

### Guide

#### Which barcode type should I use?

| Type | What it encodes | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Code 128 | Letters, digits and symbols | Shipping labels, inventory, asset tags, tickets |
| EAN-13 | 12 digits + check digit | Retail products worldwide; books (ISBN, starting 978/979) |
| EAN-8 | 7 digits + check digit | Small retail packs |
| UPC-A | 11 digits + check digit | Retail products in the US and Canada |
| UPC-E | Compressed 6-digit UPC | Small items sold in the US and Canada |
| Code 39 | A–Z, 0–9 and - . $ / + % space | Industry, defence and older systems |
| ITF-14 | 13 digits + check digit (GTIN-14) | Outer shipping cartons |
| Codabar | Digits and - $ : / . + | Libraries, blood banks, couriers |
| GS1-128 | GS1 data such as GTIN, batch and expiry | Pallet and logistics labels, pharma |
| Data Matrix | Any text, in a small square | Small parts, electronics, medicines |
| PDF417 | Large amounts of text | ID cards, boarding passes, permits |

#### Getting a real EAN or UPC number for your product

Barcodes on products sold in shops and on most marketplaces must use a number licensed from **GS1** — in India, from GS1 India. The number identifies your company and product worldwide, so it can’t simply be made up.

This generator draws any number to the standard, and is ideal once you have your GS1 numbers, or for internal barcodes where you choose the numbering: stock-keeping units, shelf labels, library books, files, assets and event tickets. For those, **Code 128** is usually the best choice.

#### Tips for barcodes that scan first time

- Print at **100% scale** — turn off “Fit to page” — so the bar width stays as you set it.
- Keep the **quiet zone** (the blank margin) on both sides; text or edges too close to the bars stop scanners.
- Use **dark bars on a light background**. Red, orange and yellow bars are invisible to red-light laser scanners.
- On a **203 DPI thermal printer** each dot is 0.125 mm, so bar widths of 0.25, 0.375 or 0.5 mm print cleanly; on 300 DPI printers use multiples of about 0.085 mm.
- Don’t shrink retail codes below 80% of standard size (a 0.26 mm bar), and avoid cutting their height.
- Scan a test print with a phone scanner app or your till scanner before printing hundreds of labels.

### Guide

#### Staying safe with QR codes

A QR code is just text, so anyone can print one — scammers stick fake codes over real ones on parking meters, posters and shop counters. Always check the website address before entering passwords or card details, and be careful with links from shorteners or bare IP addresses.

For **UPI** payments, check that the name your UPI app shows matches who you want to pay. You never need to scan a QR code or enter your UPI PIN to **receive** money — that’s a common fraud.

#### If a code won’t scan

Move closer so the code fills most of the frame, hold steady, and add light — on phones, tap **Light** for the torch. For images, use a sharp, straight photo or a screenshot rather than a photo of a screen.

### Guide

#### What the GS1 prefix tells you

The first digits of an EAN-13 show which national GS1 office gave the company its barcode numbers — 890 for GS1 India, 400–440 for Germany, 690–699 for China. They **don’t** prove where the product was made: a company registered in India can make goods anywhere, and vice versa.

Codes starting with 20–29 are shops’ own numbers (loose produce, weighed items), and 978/979 are books.
