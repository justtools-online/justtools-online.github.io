Snakes and Ladders

The classic race to 100 for two to four players: roll the die, climb the ladders and dodge the snakes.

At a glance

  • Play 2, 3 or 4 players — you against the computer, or friends taking turns on one phone, tablet or computer.
  • Land on the foot of a ladder to climb it; land on a snake’s head and slide down to its tail. First to square 100 wins.
  • Choose a board: Classic, Easy climb for younger players, the longer Snake pit, or a Random board that is new every game.
  • Set your house rules: bounce back, stay put or still win when you roll past 100, another roll on a six, and a six to start.

Step by step

How to play Snakes and Ladders

  1. 1

    Roll the die

    Tap the die or press Space. Your token moves forward that many squares, starting from off the board.

  2. 2

    Climb ladders

    If you finish on the foot of a ladder, climb straight up to its top.

  3. 3

    Watch out for snakes

    If you finish on a snake’s head, slide down to its tail.

  4. 4

    Roll a six

    A six gives you another roll (you can turn this rule off).

  5. 5

    Reach 100

    The first player to reach square 100 wins. Choose what happens if you roll past it.

House rules for square 100

  • Bounce back (the default): roll too high and you count up to 100 and back again — on 97, a 5 takes you to 98.
  • Stay put: you need the exact number; if the roll is too high, you don’t move.
  • Still wins: any roll that reaches 100 wins, which makes games shorter.
  • A study in The Mathematical Gazette (1993) worked out game lengths this way, treating the board as a Markov chain — the same maths shows the average on each board here.

About Snakes and Ladders

Snakes and Ladders began in India as part of a family of dice board games that also includes Pachisi, the ancestor of Ludo. Known as Moksha Patam, it was a lesson in morals: ladders were virtues such as generosity and humility, snakes were vices such as anger and theft, and reaching the top meant liberation (moksha). The Jain version is called Gyan Chaupar.

The game reached England in the 1890s — the earliest known English version was registered in 1892 — and later the United States, where it is sold as Chutes and Ladders. In Hindi it is called Saanp Seedhi, in Telugu Vaikunthapali and in Tamil Parama Padam. The phrase “back to square one” is thought to come from it.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Can I play Snakes and Ladders with the computer?

Yes. Choose Computer and play Red against one to three computer players, who roll by themselves.

Can I play with friends?

Yes, on one device: choose Friends (pass & play) and two to four players take turns.

What happens if you roll more than you need to reach 100?

You choose: bounce back off 100 (the default), stay where you are, or win anyway.

Do you need a six to start?

Not by default — everyone starts off the board and moves straight away. Switch on Need a six to start for that house rule.

How long does a game take?

Each board shows the average number of rolls one player needs, worked out from the board and your rules — around 38 on the Classic board with bounce-back.

Is my game saved?

Yes — the game in progress and your record against the computer are saved in this browser.
