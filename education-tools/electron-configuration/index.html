Electron Configuration Builder

Pick any of the 118 elements, or type an ion such as Fe3+, to see its electron configuration, orbital diagram and quantum numbers — then switch to Practice and fill the orbitals yourself.

Runs in your browser Free · no sign-up Works on any device

At a glance

  • Gives the ground-state electron configuration of all 118 elements, in full (1s² 2s² 2p⁶ …) and in noble-gas shorthand ([Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²).
  • Handles ions from 4− to 8+: electrons leave the outermost shell first, so Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s².
  • Includes the 20 elements that break the filling order, such as chromium ([Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹) and copper ([Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹), and shows what the rule would have predicted.
  • Checked against the NIST Atomic Spectra Database for every atom from hydrogen to hassium and every positive ion of those elements up to 8+.
  • Draws the orbital diagram with Hund’s rule, and can fill it one electron at a time.
  • Lists electrons per shell, valence electrons, unpaired electrons, whether the atom is paramagnetic or diamagnetic, its block, period and group, and the quantum numbers of the last electron.
  • Practice mode: fill the boxes yourself and get told which rule — Aufbau, Pauli or Hund — the first mistake breaks.
  • Free, with nothing to install; works on a phone.

Features

Everything you need, nothing you don’t

Atoms and ions

Type Fe, iron, 26 or Fe3+, or click the periodic table and step the charge up and down.

Both ways of writing it

By shell ([Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²), as most textbooks and data tables print it, or in filling order ([Ar] 4s² 3d⁶), as many classes teach it.

Exceptions explained

For chromium, copper, silver, gold and the rest, the measured configuration is shown next to the predicted one, so you see exactly what differs.

A diagram you can build

Click orbitals to add and pair electrons. The check points to the first subshell that is wrong and explains the rule behind it.

Answers the follow-up questions

How many valence electrons? How many unpaired? Paramagnetic? What are n, l, mℓ and ms of the last electron? They are all on the page.

Backed by measured data

Configurations follow NIST’s published ground states, not just a rule — including the awkward 1+ ions of vanadium, cobalt and nickel.

The three rules

  • Aufbau principle — electrons fill the lowest-energy subshell that has room, in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.
  • Pauli exclusion principle — an orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins. That makes the capacities s = 2, p = 6, d = 10, f = 14.
  • Hund’s rule — within a subshell, every orbital gets one electron before any orbital gets a second.

Elements that break the filling order

Twenty elements have a ground state the filling order does not predict: Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, La, Ce, Gd, Pt, Au, Ac, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm and Lr. In most of them an electron moves from the s subshell into d, because a half-filled or filled d subshell is slightly more stable — chromium is 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁴ 4s², and copper 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁹ 4s².

These cannot be worked out from the rule; they are measured. The tool shows the measured configuration and, beside it, what the rule alone would give.

Why ions lose 4s before 3d

In a neutral atom 4s fills before 3d. Once 3d holds electrons, though, it lies lower in energy than 4s, so when a transition metal becomes a positive ion the 4s electrons leave first. Iron, [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², becomes Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵. Writing Fe²⁺ as [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² — the configuration of a neutral atom with 24 electrons — is the commonest mistake, and Practice mode points it out.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is the electron configuration of iron?

Iron (atomic number 26) is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², or [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² in short. It has 4 unpaired electrons. Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.

Should 4s or 3d be written first?

Both are accepted. Writing by shell — [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² — is the convention of NIST and most reference tables. Writing in filling order — [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ — is common in school courses. Use the switch under the result to match your textbook.

How many valence electrons does an element have?

For main-group elements (groups 1, 2 and 13–18) it is the number of s and p electrons in the outermost shell, and the tool shows it. For transition metals and the f-block the word has no single definition, because inner d or f electrons also take part in bonding, so the tool shows the outer-shell count and says so.

What are the quantum numbers of the last electron?

They describe the final electron added: n is its shell, l its subshell (0 = s, 1 = p, 2 = d, 3 = f), mℓ its orbital and ms its spin. The tool numbers orbitals from −l to +l and puts spin-up electrons in first, the convention most textbooks use; a few courses number them the other way round.

Why is my answer for Cr or Cu marked wrong in Practice?

Chromium and copper are exceptions: their ground states are 3d⁵ 4s¹ and 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. If you enter what the filling order predicts, the check tells you that it is the predicted configuration and what the real one is.

How reliable are the configurations of the heaviest elements?

From hydrogen to hassium (element 108) they are the ground states listed by NIST. For meitnerium to oganesson only a few atoms have ever been made, so the configurations are predictions from the filling order, and the tool labels them as such.

Which ions can it show?

Charges from 4− to 8+, which covers the ions met in chemistry. Positive ions follow NIST’s measured ground states; for negative ions the extra electrons are placed by the filling order.
